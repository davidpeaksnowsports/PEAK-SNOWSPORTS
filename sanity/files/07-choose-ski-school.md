A ski lesson is the single most leveraged purchase you make on a ski holiday. Get the right one, and you transform your week — and possibly your whole skiing life. Get the wrong one, and you spend five days frustrated, stuck on terrain that's too easy or too hard, with an instructor who isn't right for you.

This isn't bad luck. Most of it is decisions you make at the booking stage. Here's how to get them right.

## Be honest about your level

The single biggest reason lessons go wrong is that people book the wrong level. Usually too high.

When a school's website says "advanced intermediate — confident on red runs, beginning to explore black", what they mean is: skis parallel at all speeds, controlled on steep terrain, can handle variable snow without falling apart. If you can do most reds but find them tiring and prefer cruising blues, you're a strong intermediate, not an advanced one.

There's no shame in booking the lower level. There's a lot of pain in booking the higher one and spending the week trying to keep up.

## Group vs private — the actual trade-offs

**Group lessons** are cheaper, more sociable, and forcing yourself to ski with strangers at a fixed level is genuinely good for your skiing. The downside: you ski at the pace of the slowest person, the instructor's attention is divided, and if the group is poorly matched, the whole week suffers.

**Private lessons** cost more per hour but typically deliver dramatically more learning per hour. The instructor focuses entirely on you, adapts to your specific issues, and you ski exactly where suits your level. For nervous skiers, mixed-ability families, or anyone with a specific goal (off-piste, moguls, a particular technique), private is almost always worth it.

The hybrid that works well: a couple of private mornings at the start of the week to fix specific issues, then group lessons or free skiing for the rest.

## ESF vs independent — the honest picture

In France, you'll see the **ESF** (École du Ski Français) everywhere — the ones in red jackets. They're the established institution and have huge capacity, which means availability when other schools are full. The quality is consistent but varies hugely by instructor; you take what you're given.

Independent schools (often international, frequently English-speaking) tend to be smaller, more selective in their hiring, and offer more flexibility. You can often request specific instructors, get more tailored lessons, and find people who've been teaching your level for years.

Neither is automatically better. Big-brand reliability vs boutique quality — pick the one that suits how you book.

## What actually matters in an instructor

- **Teaching qualification, not just skiing qualification.** Lots of brilliant skiers are mediocre instructors. Look for full ISIA stamp at minimum, or BASI Level 3/4 for British qualifications.
- **Experience with your level.** A former World Cup racer is wasted on a nervous beginner. An instructor who's spent ten years specialising in adult beginners is gold.
- **Language fluency.** "Speaks English" can mean anything from "barely" to "fluently". For technical instruction, you want fluently.
- **Reviews from skiers like you.** A glowing review from an expert means nothing to a beginner and vice versa.

## Red flags to watch for

- A school that won't tell you who your instructor will be until you arrive.
- "Mixed level" groups that combine beginners and intermediates.
- Lessons that move groups together regardless of progression.
- Anyone who pressures you to book longer lessons than you asked about.
- Vague answers about cancellation policies or weather refunds.

## Book early

This is the unglamorous truth: the best instructors in popular resorts are booked solid by October or November for peak weeks (Christmas, half-term, February school holidays). If you're skiing in those weeks, you need to book months ahead.

For off-peak weeks (early December, mid-January, March outside school holidays), you've got more flexibility, but the principle stands: book the school before you book the chalet.

## The bottom line

The difference between a great instructor and an average one isn't 10%. It's the difference between a transformative week and a forgettable one. Spend the time getting this right.
