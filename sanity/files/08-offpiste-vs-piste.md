Every winter, we watch the same scene play out. A group books Verbier, or Chamonix, or St Anton because they've read it's the best resort in the Alps. They arrive. They look at the terrain. They spend the week stuck on the few groomed pistes, looking nervously at the off-piste fields all around them, wondering why everyone said this was the best skiing.

This is the resort-choice mistake — confusing famous off-piste resorts with universally good resorts. They're not the same thing.

## What "best resort" actually means

There's no objectively best ski resort. There are resorts that suit different skiers. A resort that's heaven for an experienced freerider might be miserable for a nervous intermediate. A family-friendly cruising paradise might bore a strong skier senseless.

The honest question isn't "what's the best resort?" — it's "what's the best resort for how I actually ski?"

## The famous freeride resorts: who they're really for

Resorts like **Verbier, Chamonix, La Grave, St Anton, Engelberg** built their reputations on off-piste terrain. The lift-served piste skiing in these resorts is often surprisingly limited — and what exists is often steep, narrow, or technical.

These resorts make sense if:
- You're a confident off-piste skier with a guide, or
- You ski genuinely advanced terrain and want to push yourself, or
- You're going for the culture and atmosphere and accept the skiing trade-offs.

They don't make sense if you ski mostly groomed pistes and want variety, easy navigation, and lots of forgiving terrain.

## Where strong intermediates actually thrive

The sweet spot for most skiers is what you might call a "linked classic" — large connected ski areas with lots of red and blue terrain, good lift infrastructure, and enough black runs to push yourself when you want to.

**The Three Valleys** (Méribel, Courchevel, Val Thorens) is the obvious one — vast, well-groomed, something for everyone. **Espace Killy** (Val d'Isère/Tignes) gives you altitude, snow reliability, and a good mix. **Paradiski** (Les Arcs/La Plagne) is underrated. **Portes du Soleil** (Morzine, Les Gets, Avoriaz) is brilliant for families and intermediates.

These resorts let you ski 30+ kilometres a day on varied terrain at your own level without ever feeling out of your depth.

## Where beginners and families thrive

True beginner-friendly resorts have specific characteristics: lots of wide, gentle blue terrain; dedicated learner areas separated from through-pistes; ski-in/ski-out access in the village; and ideally, terrain that's intuitive rather than confusing.

**Les Gets, La Tania, La Rosière, Alpe d'Huez, Les Saisies** all fit this profile. **Avoriaz** is excellent for families thanks to its car-free village. **Söll and Söll-Going** in Austria are quietly some of the best beginner resorts in Europe.

What to avoid as a beginner: resorts where the village base is also where the steep terrain is, resorts requiring complex lift navigation, and any resort marketed primarily on off-piste.

## How to read piste maps honestly

Resort piste maps are marketing documents. Treat them with appropriate scepticism.

- Count the genuine beginner pistes — not "easy blues" but actual greens and very gentle blues. Some "family" resorts have surprisingly few.
- Look at where the lifts deposit you. If every lift unloads you onto red runs, that's not actually a beginner resort.
- Notice connection routes — some resorts force intermediates to use difficult runs to navigate between areas, even though the terrain itself isn't difficult.

## The snobbery problem

There's a status hierarchy in skiing that doesn't serve most skiers. Verbier and Chamonix sound impressive. Les Gets and Söll don't. But Les Gets and Söll might be where you'd actually have the better holiday.

The skiers who have the best holidays are the ones who pick the resort that matches their skiing, not the resort that impresses their friends. Be honest about how you ski, choose accordingly, and you'll be the one with the great week.

## The exception

If you're booking lessons with a serious instructor in a freeride resort — for example, a private guide in Verbier or off-piste lessons in Chamonix — the calculation changes. You're using the resort's terrain advantage with the support to access it safely. That's exactly what those resorts are for.

For everyone else, pick the resort that matches the skiing you actually do.
